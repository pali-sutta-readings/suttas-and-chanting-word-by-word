# Learning the Word-by-Word Meaning of the Chanting

## How to use word-by-word translations {.unnumbered}

These resources can be used in a number of ways to enhance the English
speaker's understanding of the Pāli chanting that is regularly chanted
by the monastic and lay communities in Western Ajahn Chah monasteries.

However, it should be clearly stated that these translations are created
by LLMs, are not scholarly, and are no replacement for the actual study
of Pāli. Readers would do well to further investigate the meaning of the
chanting using tools such as
[Simsapa](https://simsapa.github.io/).

That being said, an effort has been made to ensure that the translations
are at least “good enough” and are, in general, a reliable reflection of
the meaning of the chanting.

For those interested in furthering their study of Pāli, a good place to
start would be the
[Pāli Sutta Readings lessons](https://pali-sutta-readings.github.io/pali-lessons/).

For those who just wish to know the meaning of a particular word in a
particular chant, it is simply a case of navigating to the relevant
chant and looking that word up. It is usually worth looking at the word
in the context of the phrase in which it appears and the various
line-by-line translations of that phrase to get a more rounded sense of
the word.

Readers may also wish to consult this
[Pāli grammar cheat sheet](https://pali-sutta-readings.github.io/assets/docs/pali-cheatsheet.pdf)
for further information on the grammar of a word.

For those who wish to learn the word-by-word meaning of the chanting so
that they understand the meaning of the chanting *as they are chanting*,
using a Spaced Repetition System (SRS) is invaluable.

In effect, an SRS is a way of using flashcards to gradually move
individual pieces of information (the meaning of a word, a grammar
point, a sutta reference, etc.) from short-term memory to long-term
memory by gradually increasing the interval between reviews of each
flashcard over a period of months.

See this
[YouTube video from Osmosis](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cVf38y07cfk&ab_channel=OsmosisfromElsevier)
or this [article by Gwern](https://gwern.net/spaced-repetition) for more
information. For a deep dive on SRSs see Gabriel Wyner's
[*Fluent Forever*](https://fluentforeverbook.com/).

There is a free SRS app called Anki which can be used on Windows,
Android, macOS, or iPhone (for iPhone there is a charge for the app,
although it is still free to use via a browser). For information on
using [Anki](https://apps.ankiweb.net/) see
[this article](https://blog.fluent-forever.com/chapter2/), which
includes links to a series of YouTube videos.

For those who don't have sufficient access to a computer and/or
smartphone, or for those who wish to avoid interacting with screens, it
is possible to make a paper-based SRS using a Leitner box.

## Making a Leitner Box

(**N.B.** The principles laid out below equally apply to the creation of
digital flashcards using an SRS app like
[Anki](https://apps.ankiweb.net/). See
[this article](https://blog.fluent-forever.com/chapter2/) for a guide on
using Anki.)

To make a Leitner box you will need:

- Plain paper (it has to be blank on both sides) cut into as many
  flashcards as you need

- A pen to write the words/translations

- A different colour pen to mark the front side of each card

- 9 envelopes or boxes in which to keep the flashcards

- A Leitner box schedule so that you know which flashcards to review
  when. You can find an example schedule in this
  [Google Sheets template](https://docs.google.com/spreadsheets/d/1ORjhGy_e_vzuwasYb86JM5cwLnc5p3TjBKoSxIRj1zk/edit?usp=sharing).

- Some kind of container to keep everything together

- Optional: paperclips to group pre-prepared flashcards and/or to keep
  the envelopes closed (you're going to end up with a lot of flashcards,
  you won't want them to fall out of the envelopes)

### Basic flashcards

Each flashcard will enable you to learn **one** piece of information.
For an unfamiliar word, that will be the meaning of that word.

::: {.keep}
Let's take the example of:

::: unit
::: {.pali lang="pi"}
Yathā vāri-vahā pūrā paripūrenti sāgaraṁ
:::

::: literal
**Literal:** Just as water-carriers full fill up the ocean
:::

::: faithful
**Faithful:** Just as rivers, when full, fill up the ocean
:::

| Word | Analysis & Meaning |
| :--- | :--- |
| **`yathā`** | `(ind)` as, just as; **[ya + thā]** |
| **`vāri-vahā`** | `(masc nom pl comp)` water-carriers, rivers; **[vāri + vaha]** |
| ↳ **`vāri`** | `(nt comp)` water; **[vāri, no root in DPD]** |
| ↳ **`vahā`** | `(masc nom pl)` carriers, those that carry; **[√vah + a]** |
| **`pūrā`** | `(masc nom pl)` full, filled; **[√pūr + a]** |
| **`paripūrenti`** | `(3rd pl pres act indic)` fill up, fill completely; **[pari + √pūr > pūre + nti]** |
| **`sāgaraṁ`** | `(masc acc sg)` ocean, sea; **[sāgara, no root in DPD]** |
:::
:::

On the front of the flashcard write the new word “Yathā”. Put a red dot
(it can be any colour) in the top left-hand corner to mark that this is
the front side of the card.

::: {.cards .single}
::: {.card .front}
Yathā
:::
:::

Now write the translation on the back of the card.

::: {.cards .single}
::: {.card}
Just as
:::
:::

To really embed the meaning of the word in your brain, you'll have to
make both Pāli-English **and** English-Pāli cards. So for “Yathā” you'll
also make:

::: {.cards .narrow}
::: {.card .front}
Just as
:::
::: {.card}
Yathā
:::
:::

If you carry on with the rest of this line, you will end up with a total
of 12 cards reading:

Yathā/ Just as - Just as/ Yathā\
vāri/ water - water/ vāri\
vahā/ rivers - rivers/ vahā\
pūrā/ full - full/ pūrā\
paripūrenti/ (they) completely fill - (they) completely fill/
paripūrenti\
sāgaraṁ/ (to the) ocean - (to the) ocean/ sāgaraṁ

**Tip:** Each flashcard can be flipped on its long or short edge. It
doesn't really matter which way you choose, but in the long run you'll
save time if you flip the cards on their long edge. Whichever way you
choose, be consistent from the start.

**Tip:** Your brain finds it much, much easier to remember images than
text, so when learning words like vahā/ rivers or sāgaraṁ/ ocean,
generate a mental image of a river or the ocean **every time** you
review it. Make sure you generate **the same** image every time. You'll
be surprised that when you come to review these cards weeks or months
later, the image will just pop into your mind, and might even start to
do so whilst you're chanting.

### Whole phrase flashcards

You can also learn the meaning of words in the context of the phrase.
This is actually more useful, as your brain will over time begin to
learn Pāli grammar in the background without you even noticing. However,
the downside is that if you're doing this by hand it takes longer to
create each flashcard. For example:

::: {.cards}
::: {.card .front}
[**Yathā**]{.u} vāri-vahā pūrā\
paripūrenti sāgaraṁ
:::
::: {.card}
[**Just as**]{.u} rivers full of water\
entirely fill up the ocean
:::
:::

::: {.cards}
::: {.card .front}
[**Just as**]{.u} rivers full of water\
entirely fill up the ocean
:::
::: {.card}
[**Yathā**]{.u} vāri-vahā pūrā\
paripūrenti sāgaraṁ
:::
:::

Another option would be:

::: {.cards}
::: {.card .front}
[ ]{.blank} vāri-vahā pūrā\
paripūrenti sāgaraṁ\
\
(Just as)
:::
::: {.card}
[**Yathā**]{.u}\
\
[**Just as**]{.u} rivers full of water\
entirely fill up the ocean
:::
:::

::: {.cards .small}
::: {.card .front}
[ ]{.blank .long} rivers, when full,\
entirely fill up the ocean
:::
::: {.card}
[**Just as**]{.u}\
\
[**Yathā**]{.u} vāri-vahā pūrā\
paripūrenti sāgaraṁ
:::
:::

### Grammar flashcards

To learn grammar, you will need a grammar guide, for example this
[Pāli grammar cheat
sheet](https://pali-sutta-readings.github.io/assets/docs/pali-cheatsheet.pdf).
Let's go back to our sentence:

**Yathā vāri-vahā pūrā paripūrenti sāgaraṁ**

Just as rivers full of water entirely fill up the ocean

The sentence is indicating that water is moving ***from*** the river
***to*** the sea. In Pāli this is indicated through the accusative case.
Checking a Pāli grammar guide, we will see that for singular masculine
nouns, the accusative is formed by adding -ṁ to the stem of the noun. To
isolate this piece of information, we need to highlight *just* the case
ending as demonstrated below:

::: {.figure}
![Four cards for the word sāgaraṁ, showing the accusative ending, in
both directions.](images/08.png){width=100%}
:::

Grammar is almost always better learnt in the context of a sentence or
phrase, so the following will work better, but take longer to write out.
However, you cannot always produce a neat English-Pāli version of the
card. You can compensate for this by making further cards that capture
the accusative case ending -ṁ from other sentences as you come across
them.

::: {.figure}
![Two cards for sāgaraṁ in the phrase, with the word underlined and “to
the ocean” in bold.](images/09.png){width=100%}
:::

**Tip:** You can add extra information to cards to capture wider
information such as the case of the word if you find that useful, for
example:

::: {.figure}
![Two cards for sāgaraṁ labelling it “(accusative)”.](images/10.png){width=100%}
:::

If we wanted to capture the third person plural present tense
conjugation of *paripūreti*, having once again consulted a Pāli grammar
guide, we could make the following cards:

::: {.figure}
![Four cards for paripūrenti and “(they) entirely fill up”, in both
directions.](images/11.png){width=100%}
:::

**Tip:** Eventually you will have a great many cards, so it can be
useful to add a context to cards so that their meaning from the chant
they appear in jumps out. For example:

::: {.figure}
![Two cards for paripūrenti with the chant context “(sāgaraṁ)”
underneath.](images/12.png){width=100%}
:::

Once again, if you are willing to put in the extra time, presenting the
conjugation in context would work best.

::: {.figure}
![Four cards for paripūrenti in context, with the English “entirely
fill up” in bold.](images/13.png){width=100%}
:::

Once you've produced all these cards, you'll be able to produce a
literal translation of the phrase and your own English rendering:

**Yathā** (just as) **vāri** (water)**-vahā** (carriers) **pūrā** (full)
**paripūrenti** (they completely fill) **sāgaraṁ** (to the ocean)

Just as the water from abundant rivers completely fill the ocean with
their water

### Reviewing the flashcards

Once you have your flashcards, they are ready to be entered into a
Leitner box. Get nine envelopes (or equivalent flashcard containers) and
label them 1 through to 7, then 8A and 8B, on the front and back. There
will be one envelope for each “review level” in the schedule. Print out
the Leitner box
[schedule](https://docs.google.com/spreadsheets/d/1ORjhGy_e_vzuwasYb86JM5cwLnc5p3TjBKoSxIRj1zk/edit?usp=sharing)
and fill in the dates. Day 1 is whatever day you are starting.

Put your prepared cards into Level 1.

**Tip:** For the first few days only enter ten cards into the system
until you get the hang of how it works. When you feel confident you can
add as many cards as you wish, but 20 is generally enough. Remember that
further down the line there will be days when many cards are reviewed so
that you will get overwhelmed if you add too many cards early on.

**Tip:** You can prepare many flashcards in advance and keep them in a
“holding envelope”. This is especially useful if you know that you've
got a busy period coming up. If you do this, it can be handy to use
paperclips to group a day's worth of cards together. Store them in such
a way that it is easy to add the groups of cards to the Level 1 envelope
in order, i.e. in the order in which the words in the cards appear in
the chant you are learning.

**Tip:** Arrange your cards so that for Level 1 you review the English
cards first to prime your brain. You're unlikely to remember the Pāli
straight away. This is easiest done as you are making the cards: just
make one pile of new Pāli cards and one pile of new English cards.

Then review the cards one by one. If you get a card correct, it moves up
a level (i.e. it goes into the next level's envelope, so if you're
reviewing a Level 1 card it moves to Level 2). If you get a card wrong,
it gets moved down a level. For Level 1 cards, keep reviewing them
until you get it right and it moves to Level 2. On any given day, use
the schedule to work out which cards you should be reviewing. Always
review cards from the highest level first, then work your way down.

Carry on like this until a card is reviewed at Level 8A or B, at which
point it can be discarded. After day 256, the Leitner schedule loops
back on itself, so the next day is day 1. You just carry on until all
your cards are successfully reviewed at Level 8A or B.

**Tip:** Do your reviews every day, even if you're busy. If you start
missing days, the reviews will start piling up, which can be
discouraging. If you do miss a day, then review all the cards from the
missed day(s) and from the current day. As ever, start with cards from
the highest level and work your way down.

**Tip:** Review every card at least until Level 7. If, having reviewed a
card at Level 7 and having got it right, you are completely confident
you've nailed its meaning, then you can discard it. Otherwise, only
discard a card when it has been correctly reviewed at Level 8A or 8B.

### Maintaining a positive attitude in the face of a daunting project

Learning the meaning of all of the Pāli chanting word-by-word is a big
task and is likely to take a while, perhaps years. Here are some tips
for maintaining a positive attitude throughout the journey:

1. **Rejoice in the ridiculous**

   People do ridiculously difficult things all the time. They run
   marathons, learn languages, learn instruments and build all sorts of
   projects from scratch into things that never before existed in the
   world. What makes them seem ridiculous is how distant they are from
   where we are now. But actually when you break them down, they are
   just a large number of small steps that need to be taken. Even if it
   were to take ten thousand flashcards to learn the word-by-word
   meaning of the chanting, the making and reviewing of each individual
   flashcard is simple. If you keep doing it, then slowly the
   ridiculous becomes the doable and then the done.

2. **Success is not having given up**

   If you define success as completion of the task, then you're failing
   until the final flashcard is reviewed at Level 8A or B. That might
   take a while. Instead just redefine success as not having given up.
   Then you're succeeding the whole way!

3. **Look back not forward**

   The end of the task is going to be in the distance for a long time,
   so you'll get discouraged if you focus on how much you have left to
   do. Instead keep looking back at what you *have* done. This will
   quickly seem to grow in just a week or two and you'll notice the
   difference when you start understanding even just part of a common
   chant as you're chanting it. So keep looking at what you *have* done
   and don't pay attention to how much you have left.

4. **Trust the process**

   After the honeymoon period where you're getting your head around the
   novelty of how the system works, it's easy to lose hope that this is
   all worth it, especially if we haven't used an SRS before. At this
   stage you just have to keep your head down and trust that the
   process will produce fruit further down the line. SRSs work. Once
   you understand the meaning of one or two common chants as you are
   chanting them, then you'll have all the evidence you need that the
   process works and that it is worth carrying on.

5. **Celebrate small wins**

   Don't save the celebration for the end of the project; celebrate
   every day! Every time you finish a flashcard-making session or a
   review session, acknowledge how well you're doing, give yourself a
   pat on the back, go for a walk, listen to a Dhamma talk, whatever.
   It sounds silly, but you'll actually start looking forward to
   attending to your Leitner box if you know that you're going to get a
   treat at the end.

6. **Keep up the momentum**

   Once you get into the rhythm of daily flashcard creation and
   reviewing, the process can seem to carry itself along. However,
   there will inevitably be a disruption to your routine (a big
   celebration in the monastery, a home visit, a retreat, etc.) that can
   knock your Leitner box off course.

   Try to never let the process come to a stop. If you need to, you can
   reduce your flashcard creation so that only a few cards are added to
   the system each day, or you can even stop adding any cards at all.
   But never stop reviewing the cards, as they will pile up and you'll
   soon get overwhelmed by the backlog. You might need to spread the
   reviews out over the day to get them in, but just keep going. In the
   absolute worst scenario, never let a day go by where you don't review
   any cards at all, even if it's just one.
